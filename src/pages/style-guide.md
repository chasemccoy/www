## Punctuation

- Em dashes are unspaced—like this. En dashes denote ranges, like January 10–16.
- Oxford comma: this, that, and the other.
- Semicolons sparingly; if "however" separates two clauses, a semicolon precedes it.
- Colons are the workhorse for introducing an elaboration or example: like this.
- Exclamation points express real excitement, never sarcasm. Used honestly, they're wonderful!
- Ellipses are three typed dots... deployed rarely, as a beat.
- Type straight quotes and apostrophes; the build curls them. Quoted material keeps whatever its source used.
- Commas and periods go inside the quotation marks, whether the quote is a complete sentence or a two-word fragment: AT stands for "authenticated transfer," and Sunday calls them "Memory Developers." American style, start to finish.
- A comma sits between two adjectives only if they could trade places. "A simple horizontal rule" takes no comma, because simple describes the whole horizontal rule.

## Capitalization

- Sentence case by default: titles, headings, everything.
- The internet and the web, lowercase. They've earned the informality.
- Brands keep their own casing: JavaScript, GitHub, YouTube.
- Common acronyms go unexplained (AI, CSS, API). Uncommon ones get a parenthetical on first use: LCARS (Library Computer Access/Retrieval System).

## Numbers

- Spell out small, casual counts (three recommendations, two kinds of tools). Use digits when precision or scale is the point (535 nm, 500 maps, 9 years at a job).
- Decades are written out: the 1990s, not the '90s.
- Percentages take digits and the symbol: 90%.

## Posts and titles

- Titles are optional. Short link posts usually go without one; a title is earned by something more composed.
- When titled: sentence case, a few words, declarative. No questions, no colon-and-subtitle constructions, no bait.
- The slug is its own channel. It can carry a message the title doesn't.
- Tags are lowercase, one to three per post.

## Links

- Link text describes its destination. Never "click here," never a naked "here."
- Start the anchor with the verb that immediately precedes the subject of the linked content, and run it to the end of the clause:

  ✅ Robin Sloan [wrote about how to end things on the internet](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/how-to-end/).

  ❌ [Robin Sloan wrote about how to end things on the internet.](https://www.robinsloan.com/notes/how-to-end/)

- When linking to a single work or term, anchor as tightly to it as possible: the reviews for ["La La Land"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_La_Land) are out.
- Don't link an entire sentence, and don't include terminal punctuation in an anchor.
- Bare URLs appear only as embeds, alone on their own line (tweets, YouTube videos).
- Link back to earlier posts when a thread continues. The archive is a conversation with itself.

## Quoting

- Long quotes get a blockquote with a cite line:

  ```markdown
  > The quoted text.
  >
  > <cite>[Title of the piece](https://example.com)</cite>
  ```

- Introduce the writer before the quote and react after it, briefly. A short quote can skip the cite line when the introduction already names the source.
- Quote people to build on them, not to argue with them.

## Formatting

- Headings appear only in long guides, and only `##` and `###`. Most posts don't need any.
- A section break between topics is a horizontal rule (`---`), not a heading.
- Italics are the emphasis tool: single-word stress, book and publication titles, coined terms.
- Bold is rare: run-in labels at the start of list items, or one key term per post. Never emphasis mid-sentence.
- Inline code for anything a computer would read literally: properties, commands, filenames, element names.
- An image's caption lives in its title string—`![alt](photo.jpg "A caption")`—and becomes the figcaption. Captions may contain markdown links for attribution. Alt text describes the image, or stays empty when the caption does the work.
- No footnotes. If it can't live in a parenthesis, it can live in the next paragraph.

## Words

- Email, but e-book.
- Website, web page, homepage.
- Open source, even as a modifier.
- Folks, as the plural of person.
- In regard to, never "in regards to."
- Cutting-edge as an adjective; the cutting edge as a noun.
- An affect is a cause (and a verb); an effect is a consequence (and a noun). Affects create effects.
- Book titles in italics, and so are blogs and publications. (An earlier draft of this guide said quotes. Practice won.)

## Aversions

Purely taste, and safe for everyone but me to ignore:

- "I.e." and "e.g."—write "that is" and "for example."
- "Etc." is tolerated, but "and more" or "and the like" is better.
- Title Case Headlines.
- Clickbait, engagement bait, and listicles without substance.
- Any language that would be at home in a corporate memo or a LinkedIn post.
